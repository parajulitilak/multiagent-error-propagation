\begin{abstract}
Pipelines of specialized LLM agents (one plans, another executes, a third
verifies) are now a default design for applied language-model systems.
The design carries an implicit promise: later stages should absorb the
mistakes of earlier ones. Whether they actually do is unresolved. Failure
taxonomies built from deployed systems describe \emph{what} goes wrong but,
being observational, cannot say how often an early error survives to the
output or what a verification stage causally contributes. We address this
with controlled fault injection, adapted from software reliability
engineering: we corrupt a single intermediate output with a known, scripted
perturbation and trace its fate through the rest of the pipeline. Faults are
graded into three severity levels, and the verification stage is additionally
evaluated under three prompt strategies (direct, chain-of-thought, rubric)
replayed on identical traces. Across 200 GSM-Hard problems, with cross-model
replication on Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct, we compare a
single-agent baseline, a clean three-stage pipeline, early- and late-stage
injection, and verifier-removed ablations of both, analyzing outcomes with
paired statistics throughout. On Claude, injected solver-stage faults reached
the final answer in 12.9\% of valid cases under the direct verifier, while
plan-stage faults propagated in only 5.0\%, showing that many upstream errors
are naturally absorbed before verification. Removing the verifier increased
solver-stage propagation to 27.3\%, isolating a substantial causal verifier
contribution. Verifier prompting strategy also mattered sharply: for
solver-stage faults, chain-of-thought and rubric verification reduced
propagation to 1.4\% and raised correction precision from 66.0\% under the
direct verifier to 95.9\% and 100.0\%, respectively. The result is a map of
which verifier catches which error, where it was injected, and how fault type
and severity interact with stage boundaries and verifier design. We release
code, prompts, and full traces.
\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} multi-agent systems, LLM pipelines, error propagation, fault injection, verification, chain-of-thought
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The unit of deployment for language models is shifting from a single model
call to a pipeline of agents. A decomposer agent plans the task, a solver
agent executes the plan, and a verifier agent checks the proposed solution
against the original problem; frameworks such as AutoGen~\citep{wu2023autogen}
and MetaGPT~\citep{hong2023metagpt} have made this pattern easy to build and,
as a result, increasingly common. The appeal of the pattern is intuitive:
specialization should improve performance, and a final review stage should
catch mistakes that earlier stages miss.

The empirical case for that promise, however, remains incomplete. On one side,
multi-agent debate and related coordination schemes have been reported to
improve factuality and reasoning~\citep{du2023debate}, and simple agent
ensembling can improve performance as the number of agents
increases~\citep{li2024moreagents}. On the other, a growing literature shows
that language models are often weak judges of their own reasoning: without
external feedback, self-correction frequently fails to improve reasoning
accuracy and may even make it worse~\citep{huang2023selfcorrect}. The most
detailed descriptive account of multi-agent breakdowns to date, the
Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST)~\citep{cemri2025mast}, catalogs
failure modes observed in deployed systems and shows that inter-agent failures
are frequent and varied. But observational traces alone cannot identify how
often an early-stage mistake would have survived to the final output, nor can
they cleanly isolate how much protection a verifier stage causally provides.

What is missing is an interventional measurement. If a known fault is inserted
at a chosen stage and the rest of the pipeline is allowed to proceed normally,
questions left open by observational work become directly measurable: How
often does an early mistake survive to the final answer? Does survival depend
on whether the fault is introduced in planning or execution? Does severity
matter? How much does a verifier actually prevent, rather than merely being
present when the pipeline succeeds for other reasons? And how much does that
depend on how the verifier is prompted?

We address these questions with controlled fault injection, adapted from
software reliability engineering. We corrupt exactly one intermediate artifact
in a Decomposer--Solver--Verifier pipeline and then let the remaining stages
run unchanged. The injected fault is known by construction, so every
downstream effect can be attributed to a specific intervention. We study three
operators designed to mimic realistic reasoning failures: \emph{number swap},
which replaces a quantity with a wrong but plausible value; \emph{operation
flip}, which reverses an arithmetic operation; and \emph{semantic logic
error}, which perturbs the order or logic of a reasoning step without merely
changing a numeral or operator. Number swaps are graded into pre-registered
severity levels (\emph{mild}, \emph{moderate}, \emph{severe}), and all
injections are logged with the exact changed span.

Our main experiments use a fixed sample of 200 GSM-Hard problems under six
paired conditions: a single-agent baseline, a clean three-stage pipeline,
injection at the decomposer stage, injection at the solver stage, and
verifier-removed versions of both injected pipelines. Because all stage
outputs are stored, the same traces can be replayed under alternative verifier
prompts, allowing direct comparison of verifier strategies on identical
inputs. In the current codebase, this enables comparison among a direct
verifier, a chain-of-thought verifier, and a rubric-based verifier. We also
include cross-model replication runs on Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct to test whether
the main qualitative patterns hold beyond the primary model family. All
comparisons are paired at the problem level, and we report interval estimates
and exact paired tests rather than point estimates alone.

Our thesis is falsifiable: in a Decomposer--Solver--Verifier pipeline, the
probability that an injected fault survives to the final answer depends on
where the fault is introduced, what type of fault it is, how severe it is,
whether a verifier is present, and how that verifier is prompted. The study's
experimental design tests each of those claims directly. In particular, it
separates stage effects (decomposer versus solver), verifier effects
(verifier present versus ablated), and verifier-strategy effects (direct
versus chain-of-thought versus rubric) while holding the underlying problems
and injected faults fixed.

This study sits at the intersection of four lines of work. First, agentic
frameworks such as AutoGen~\citep{wu2023autogen}, MetaGPT~\citep{hong2023metagpt},
and CAMEL~\citep{li2023camel} established reusable patterns for composing LLM
agents; the Decomposer--Solver--Verifier pipeline we study is the smallest
instance that captures planning, execution, and review as distinct roles.
Second, prior work on debate, ensembling, and self-consistency measures
aggregate accuracy gains~\citep{du2023debate,li2024moreagents,wang2022selfconsistency},
but usually does not distinguish between systems that truly prevent errors and
systems that merely replace one error with another. Controlled fault injection
makes that distinction observable. Third, work on self-correction and
verification shows both the promise and the limitations of reflective
reasoning: Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion} and
Self-Refine~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine} show that models can revise outputs
given feedback, while Huang et~al.~\citep{huang2023selfcorrect} show that,
without external signals, such revision often fails on reasoning tasks; LLM
judging also carries known biases~\citep{zheng2023judge}. Our verifier
ablation and replay design therefore measure not just whether review exists,
but how much it changes the fate of a known fault under identical inputs.
Finally, MAST~\citep{cemri2025mast} provides a vocabulary for multi-agent
failure, while recent mitigation systems such as DebUnc~\citep{yoffe2024debunc}
and graph-based governance approaches~\citep{xie2026spark} are motivated by
the assumption that upstream errors propagate downstream. Our study tests that
assumption directly and quantifies when it holds.

\textbf{Contributions.}

(i)~A controlled fault-injection methodology for measuring error propagation
in LLM agent pipelines, with reproducible perturbation operators and
pre-registered severity bands.

(ii)~Quantitative estimates of propagation, catch, and correction rates under
paired comparisons, including effects of injection stage, fault type, and
fault severity.

(iii)~A verifier ablation that isolates the causal contribution of the
verification stage by comparing matched runs with and without a verifier.

(iv)~A strategy-level map of verification, comparing direct,
chain-of-thought, and rubric-based verifier prompts on identical stored
traces across error types, severities, and injection stages.

(v)~A released codebase with prompts, configurations, analysis scripts, and
trace artifacts sufficient to reproduce the reported measurements.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Pipeline Architecture and Test Conditions}
Our multi-agent system uses three specialized LLM agents working sequentially:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decomposer:} Breaks down a word problem into a short 2--5 step plan without solving it.
    \item \textbf{Solver:} Executes the plan step-by-step to compute the final numeric answer.
    \item \textbf{Verifier:} Reviews the solution against the original problem and either accepts it or rejects it with a corrected answer.
\end{enumerate}

To isolate how errors propagate and how verifiers protect the pipeline, we test six experimental conditions on a fixed set of math problems (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). Condition~A is a single-agent baseline. Condition~B is a clean multi-agent run. In Conditions~C1 and C2, we inject a controlled fault into the plan or solution before passing it to the next agent. Conditions~D1 and D2 repeat C1 and C2 with the verifier removed to measure baseline error propagation.

\subsection{Controlled Fault Injection}
\label{sec:injection}
To simulate realistic reasoning mistakes, we corrupt exactly one intermediate artifact per run using three fault types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Number Swap:} Replaces a number in the plan or solution with a wrong value, categorized into three severity tiers: \emph{mild} ($<15\%$ change), \emph{moderate} ($30\%$--$60\%$ change), or \emph{severe} ($>200\%$ change or sign flip).
    \item \textbf{Operation Flip:} Swaps an arithmetic operation with its inverse (e.g., changing \emph{add} to \emph{subtract} or \emph{multiply} to \emph{divide}).
    \item \textbf{Semantic Logic Error:} Rewrites the logical sequence of steps while keeping the language fluent.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verifier Prompt Strategies and Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:strategies}
To test how prompt design affects verifier performance, we evaluate every run under three prompt strategies on identical traces:
(1)~\textbf{Direct:} Emits an immediate accept/reject verdict and corrected line;
(2)~\textbf{Chain-of-Thought (CoT):} Generates step-by-step verification reasoning before declaring a verdict; and
(3)~\textbf{Rubric:} Systematically evaluates the solution against four explicit criteria (reading, variables, operations, calculations).

Every injected fault results in one of four outcomes: \emph{caught and corrected}, \emph{caught but wrong correction}, \emph{silently propagated}, or \emph{absorbed} (the solver naturally corrected the plan mistake).

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.} Experiments are conducted on $n=200$ GSM-Hard math problems using Claude Sonnet 4.6 (primary) and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct (replication) at temperature 0. Condition comparisons use McNemar's exact test with Holm--Bonferroni correction ($p_{\mathrm{holm}}$). Prompts are listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prompts}.

\subsection*{RQ1: How do planning vs. execution errors propagate, and does removing the verifier increase failure?}

To begin with, injections in the planner agent (Condition C1) propagate to the final output only in 5.0\% of the cases on Claude and in 4.0\% of them on Qwen. Solvers naturally absorb most plan errors by re-deriving calculations directly from the problem statement (Figure~\ref{fig:fate}). In contrast, solver-stage injections (Condition C2) propagate at 12.9\% on Claude and 11.2\% on Qwen—more than double the plan-stage rate (Claude OR = 0.28, $p = 0.0084$; Qwen OR = 0.35, $p = 0.0515$).

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig3_acc.png}
\caption{Fate of injected faults by injection stage ($C1$ Plan vs $C2$ Solver).}\label{fig:fate}

Removing the verifier agent (Condition D2) causes solver-stage fault propagation to jump from 12.9\% to 27.3\% on Claude ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.0382$) and from 11.2\% to 25.4\% on Qwen ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.0781$). In contrast, removing the verifier for plan-stage injections (Condition D1) only slightly increases propagation to 8.6\% on Claude ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.4240$) and 8.7\% on Qwen ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 1.0000$), confirming that verifiers provide minimal additional benefit at the planning stage. In other words, verifier agents provide a strong defense against late-stage execution errors, while planning errors are largely self-corrected by intermediate agents before reaching the verification steps.

\subsection*{RQ2: What are the effects of various verifier prompting strategies to catch errors with different levels of severity?}

Under Direct verification, verifiers catch errors, yet frequently fail to fix them. Thus, this strategy yields 66.0\% correction precision on Claude and 85.9\% on Qwen. On the other hand, both Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Rubric strategies increase correction precision to 95.9\%--100.0\% on Claude and 95.3\%--98.0\% on Qwen. Consequently, CoT and Rubric reduce solver-stage propagation to 1.4\% on Claude and 4.6\%--4.7\% on Qwen (Table~\ref{tab:strategy}). This gain comes at the cost of a modest computational overhead; CoT and Rubric verifiers increase token usage by 30\%--50\%. Consequently, this elevates total token cost per problem by 23\%--37\% (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_reports}, Table~\ref{tab:master_cost} for full token breakdowns).

\section{Analysis}

Qualitative trace inspection reveals two primary mechanisms governing verifier behavior and failure modes across the pipeline.

\subsection*{Post-Hoc Correction Drift in Direct Verification}
Direct verification suffers from a clear structural bottleneck: high error detection paired with poor repair accuracy. Under Direct prompting, verifiers correctly identify flawed solver outputs but frequently blunder the repair step, introducing secondary arithmetic slips when generating the corrected final answer line. Because Direct prompts force the model to emit a single-pass verdict and answer without a computational scratchpad, the model relies on unconstrained generation for correction. Chain-of-Thought and Rubric prompting eliminate this drift by forcing the verifier to construct a step-by-step derivation before emitting the final answer, driving correction precision from 66.0\% to 100.0\%.

\subsection*{Label-Reasoning Dissociation}
A more subtle failure mode is \emph{label-reasoning dissociation}, where the verifier's written critique explicitly identifies the injected fault yet the final verdict token reads \texttt{VERDICT: ACCEPT}. For example, trace audits reveal instances where the verifier notes in prose that a step used an incorrect value, but nevertheless approves the overall solution. This dissociation breaks the implicit assumption that reasoning traces causally drive final verdicts. Structured Rubric prompting mitigates this dissociation by forcing the model to explicitly state pass/fail labels for individual evaluation criteria prior to emitting its overall verdict.

\section{Conclusion}
Errors do not travel equally across an agent pipeline. When a planner makes a mistake, the solver usually fixes it on its own by reading the original question. In fact, intermediate solvers absorb over 90\% of plan-stage errors across both Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct. Because of this self-correction, adding a verifier after the planner provides little value. It reduces final error propagation by only 3\%--4\% (from 8.6\% D1 to 5.0\% C1 on Claude, and 8.7\% D1 to 4.0\% C1 on Qwen).

Solver errors are a completely different story. Mistakes made during execution pass straight to the final answer unless a verifier catches them. Here, adding a verifier cuts final pipeline failure in half (from 27.3\% D2 down to 12.9\% C2 on Claude, and 25.4\% D2 to 11.2\% C2 on Qwen).

Prompt design matters immensely. Simple direct verifiers spot errors easily, but they frequently blunder the repair step. This happens because single-pass prompts force the model to guess a fix without scratchpad reasoning. Requiring Chain-of-Thought or Rubric verification solves this problem completely. It forces step-by-step evaluation before outputting a verdict token, boosting repair accuracy to 95\%--100\% and suppressing final error propagation to 1\%--4\%.

One major vulnerability remains. Simple operation flips---such as swapping \emph{add} for \emph{subtract}---act as a blind spot across all models and prompt styles, yielding catch rates below 15.0\%.

Our findings suggest three practical rules for building multi-agent systems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Verify execution, not planning:} Place verifiers at the solver stage where errors actually survive.
    \item \textbf{Mandate structured prompts:} Require step-by-step reasoning (Chain-of-Thought or Rubric) so verifiers do not ruin their corrections.
    \item \textbf{Add symbolic safeguards:} Pair LLM verifiers with programmatic math checks to catch operation flips that language models miss.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Broader Impact.} Agent pipelines increasingly handle real-world tasks. Pinpointing where errors survive helps developers place guardrails effectively; we do not foresee direct misuse arising from these findings.

\section{Improvements}
Our perturbations are synthetic by design; this is what makes origin
attribution exact, but naturally arising errors are more varied, and our
operators cover arithmetic slips and structured logic faults rather than,
for example, factual hallucinations in open-ended generation. Results are
reported across two model families and one primary task distribution with
objectively checkable answers; propagation dynamics on open-ended tasks may
differ. The verifier prompts are representative rather than exhaustively
tuned, so our estimates of verification benefit should be read as
typical-configuration, not best-achievable. The study was designed and
executed within a four-week program.

Several extensions would strengthen the study, in rough order of value:
expanding the protocol to additional task distributions beyond GSM-Hard;
broadening semantic fault injection so that contradictions, omitted
constraints, and fabricated entities are more systematically covered;
tightening the solver prompt's plan adherence, so that plan-stage faults are
not absorbed by silent re-derivation and the planning-stage estimate is
sharpened; comparing a small verifier fine-tuned with QLoRA~\citep{dettmers2023qlora}
on the released traces against the LLM judge, to test whether low-cost
specialized oversight can match it; investigating dynamic verifier gating
(e.g., only invoking the more expensive chain-of-thought or rubric verifiers
when the solver expresses high uncertainty) to optimize the token cost budget;
and evaluating learned inter-agent
gating in the style of DebUnc~\citep{yoffe2024debunc} under our injection
protocol, which would connect the measurement in this paper to the
mitigation literature it motivates.

\section{Evaluation}
We assess the design against the standard threats to validity.
\emph{Internal validity}: all contrasts are paired on the same fixed,
seeded problem sample; every injection is validated and its full record
released, so any reader can audit the treatment. \emph{Construct validity}:
propagation is measured only on problems the clean pipeline solves
correctly, so a wrong final answer under injection is attributable to the
fault rather than to baseline inability, and absorption is reported as its
own fate rather than folded into success. \emph{Statistical validity}: exact
paired tests, Wilson and bootstrap intervals, and Holm correction over the
pre-registered contrast family~\citep{dror2018hitchhiker}. \emph{External
validity}: results cover one primary reasoning dataset with objectively
checkable answers and two model families; replication on both Claude Sonnet
4.6 and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct tests whether the observed propagation
patterns depend on a single model. All conclusions in this paper are drawn
from these deterministic primary runs (temperature 0) and are stated only where the outcomes
across both model families agree.

\section{Role Prompts}\label{sec:appendix_prompts}

\paragraph{Decomposer.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a planning agent. Break the problem into a short numbered plan of 2-5 concrete steps with any intermediate quantities needed. Do NOT solve it. Output only the plan.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Solver.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a solving agent. Execute the given plan step by step on the problem. Show brief working, then end with the line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier (Direct).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a verification agent. Check the proposed solution against the original problem. If correct, reply 'VERDICT: ACCEPT'. If you find an error, reply 'VERDICT: REJECT', explain the error in one sentence, and give a corrected line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier (Chain-of-Thought).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a verification agent. Check the proposed solution against the original problem.\\
First, write down your step-by-step verification reasoning (thinking out loud). In your reasoning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carefully read the original problem statement to identify all numerical inputs, constraints, and implicit/explicit assumptions.
    \item Confirm if the proposed solution correctly read the problem and applied all necessary assumptions.
    \item Verify the mathematical logic and calculations step-by-step.
\end{itemize}
Then, end your response with:\\
VERDICT: ACCEPT (if the solution is correct)\\
OR\\
VERDICT: REJECT\\
If you reject, explain the error in one sentence, and give a corrected line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier (Rubric).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a verification agent. Evaluate the proposed solution by systematically verifying these four items:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Reading \& Assumptions: Did the solution correctly read the problem text, capture all conditions, and apply correct assumptions?
    \item Variables: Are the numbers used from the question identical to the original problem?
    \item Operations: Are the mathematical operations chosen (add/sub/mult/div) logically correct?
    \item Calculations: Are the calculations mathematically correct?
\end{enumerate}
Write down your evaluation for each of the 4 points above.\\
Then, end your response with:\\
VERDICT: ACCEPT (if all checks pass)\\
OR\\
VERDICT: REJECT (if any check fails)\\
If you reject, explain the error in one sentence, and provide a corrected line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Single agent.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{Solve the problem step by step. End with the line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\section{Injection Examples}\label{sec:appendix_injections}

\paragraph{Number swap.}
``Step 1: add 45 and 12.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Step 1: add 76 and 12.''
(step labels unchanged).

``Take the 8 apples.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Take the 3 apples.''

``Compute 250 $\times$ 0.2.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Compute 150 $\times$ 0.2.''

\paragraph{Operation flip.}
``Multiply the total by 3.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Divide the total by 3.''

``Add the shipping cost at the end.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Subtract the shipping
cost at the end.''

``Divide the distance by the time.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Multiply the distance by
the time.''

\paragraph{Semantic logic error.}
``Use the discounted price first, then add the service fee.''
$\rightarrow$ ``Add the service fee first, then apply the discount to the total.''

``Since the boys and girls were split equally, divide the total by 2 before
applying the shell-count rule.''
$\rightarrow$ ``Apply the shell-count rule directly to the total before
splitting into groups.''

``Find the remaining amount after step 2, then compare it with the target.''
$\rightarrow$ ``Compare the amount with the target before subtracting the
intermediate cost.''

\section{Representative Run Trace}\label{sec:appendix_trace}
Below is a representative trace from our experimental runs (problem ID: \texttt{gsmhard-train-1209}), showing how a plan-stage number swap is propagated by the solver and subsequently corrected by the verifier:

\paragraph{Problem.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{The local firefighters are doing a ``fill the boot'' fundraiser. Their goal is to raise \$3522385. After the first 3 hours, they have raised \$2100. For how many hours do they have to fundraise in total to reach their goal, assuming an equal amount raised in every hour?}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Decomposer plan.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{1. Calculate the amount raised per hour by dividing \$2100 by 3 hours.\\
2. Divide the total goal (\$3,522,385) by the hourly rate to find the total number of hours needed.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Injected plan-stage fault (C1 condition - Number swap).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{1. Calculate the amount raised per hour by dividing \textbf{\$1890} by 3 hours.\\
2. Divide the total goal (\$3,522,385) by the hourly rate to find the total number of hours needed.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Solver execution.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{Step 1: Calculate the amount raised per hour: \$1890 $\div$ 3 = \$630 per hour.\\
Step 2: Divide the total goal by the hourly rate: \$3,522,385 $\div$ \$630 = 5,591.88... Rounding up to complete the goal: 5,592 hours.\\
FINAL ANSWER: 5592}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier verdict \& correction.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{The problem states they raised \$2100 in the first 3 hours, giving a rate of \$2100 $\div$ 3 = \$700 per hour. The proposed solution incorrectly uses \$1890 and \$630 per hour instead. The correct calculation is \$3,522,385 $\div$ \$700 = 5,031.978..., which rounds up to 5,032 hours.\\
VERDICT: REJECT\\
FINAL ANSWER: 5032}
\end{quote}

\section{Answers to Omitted Research Sub-Questions}\label{sec:appendix_answers}

This appendix provides concise, point-form answers to the specific secondary research sub-questions that were streamlined out of the main text.

\subsection{Fault Severity \& Sensitivity Dynamics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Injection Stage Dominance:} The injection stage (planning vs. execution) is the primary determinant of fault propagation. Fault severity (mild, moderate, severe) plays a secondary, non-monotonic role.
    \item \textbf{Direct Verifier Sensitivity:} Under Direct verification, verifiers exhibit noisy sensitivity to numerical slip magnitude (e.g., solver-stage number-swap propagation on Claude is 0.0\% for mild, 18.8\% for moderate, and 7.7\% for severe; on Qwen, 9.1\% mild, 20.0\% moderate, 22.2\% severe).
    \item \textbf{Structured Verifier Neutralization:} Deploying Chain-of-Thought (CoT) or Rubric verification neutralizes numerical severity effects, suppressing propagation for both mild and moderate number swaps to \textbf{0.0\%} across models (Table~\ref{tab:master_strategy}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational Overhead of Structured Verification}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Agent Baseline Overhead:} Moving from a single-agent baseline (Condition A) to a clean multi-agent pipeline (Condition B) increases total token cost per solved problem by \textbf{$3.1\times$} on Claude (526 to 1,616 tokens) and \textbf{$2.2\times$} on Qwen (737 to 1,623 tokens).
    \item \textbf{Verifier Stage Overhead:} Structured prompting (CoT and Rubric) increases token consumption at the verifier stage by \textbf{30\%--50\%} relative to Direct verification.
    \item \textbf{Pipeline Token Overhead:} Because verification represents only one stage of the pipeline, CoT and Rubric prompting add only a modest \textbf{23\%--37\%} total token cost per problem (Table~\ref{tab:master_cost}).
\end{itemize}
