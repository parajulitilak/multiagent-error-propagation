\begin{abstract}
Pipelines involving dedicated LLM agents for planning, execution, and verification have 
become the standard setup for applied language-model systems. Such pipelines entail an 
implicit assumption: downstream stages can compensate for any errors made earlier. It is 
unclear whether this assumption is justified. Failure taxonomies built based on real-world 
systems describe symptoms of failure (the “what”) but, due to their observational nature, do
not allow one to estimate how often an early error propagates all the way to the output or 
what the causal role of verification is. We fill this gap using a technique borrowed from 
software reliability engineering—controlled fault injection: a single intermediate output 
is manipulated in a prescribed way, and its evolution is followed through the pipeline. 
Numeric faults are graded into three severity tiers, and the verification stage is assessed
under three prompting techniques (direct, chain-of-thought, and rubric), each re-run on the
same set of traces. On 200 GSM-Hard problems, with cross-model replication on Claude Sonnet 4.6
and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct, we contrast the single-agent baseline with clean and faulty three-stage
pipelines, as well as ablations of the verifier on each stage. With the verifier in place,
injected faults rarely survive on Claude: 2.9\% of valid injections reach the final answer
from either stage under the direct verifier. Removing the verifier raises execution-stage
propagation to 27.3\%, isolating a large causal verifier contribution ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} <
0.0001$), while the corresponding planning-stage change is small and not significant---most
upstream errors are absorbed by the solver before verification ever runs. The Qwen
replication reproduces the direction but not the magnitude, and it is there that verifier
prompting matters most: direct prompting repairs only 78.5\% of the faults it catches and
leaves 15.9\% propagating, whereas chain-of-thought and rubric prompting reach 95.3\%--98.0\%
repair precision and 4.6\%--4.7\% propagation. On Claude all three strategies already repair
95.7\%--100.0\% at the solver stage, so structured verification prompts pay for themselves mainly when the
verifier is the weak link. We rely on paired statistical analysis throughout, and release
code, prompts, and trace data.
\newline
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} multi-agent systems, LLM pipelines, error propagation, fault injection, 
verification, chain-of-thought
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models are now becoming progressively modular, using different components to 
complete tasks, rather than relying on a single call to a model. This includes a decomposer 
agent, a solver agent to complete the plan, and a verifier agent to check the solution. 
Building frameworks such as AutoGen~\citep{wu2023autogen} and MetaGPT~\citep{hong2023metagpt} 
enable the construction of these modular systems. Utilizing modular components provides space 
for specialization and includes an additional checkpoint to catch mistakes. However, the 
actual performance of this modularity remains to be fully tested. Multi-agent debate, and 
other coordination strategies, were reported to improve both reasoning and factuality~\citep{du2023debate}, 
while a simple agent ensemble has been shown to improve efficacy as the number of agents in the 
ensemble increases~\citep{li2024moreagents}. 
\newline A large amount of literature also points to language models being poor judges of 
their own reasoning. Without any kind of external input, self-correction is unlikely to 
improve reasoning accuracy and is likely to decrease it~\citep{huang2023selfcorrect}. The 
most extensive account of multi-agent system breakdowns to this date is the Multi-Agent 
System Failure Taxonomy~\citep{cemri2025mast}, which details the different failures of 
deployed systems. It particularly focuses on the failures that stem from the interaction of 
different agents. It provides clear evidence for the varied and extensive nature of failures. 
However, this solely provides evidence of the frequency of mistakes in the early stages of 
multi-agent systems. It cannot provide any evidence for how often mistakes went unchecked in 
the final output, nor can it provide any evidence how much of a buffer the verifier agent 
provides.
What lacks is an interventional measure. If an acknowledged fault is incorporated somewhere 
along the line and the remainder of the pipeline is allowed to operate as normal, many of the 
limitations of observational studies are resolved. What is being measured is the frequency 
that an early error is retained in the final product. What is being measured is whether 
retention of the error depends on the stage of the fault (planning vs execution), whether 
retention depends on fault severity, and what the verifier actually prevents or removes. 
Is the verifier only present when the pipeline is successful due to other external factors? 
How does the role of the verifier change based on how the verifier is prompted?

To measure these, we have adapted a method from software reliability called fault injection. 
We will purposely introduce a fault to one of the steps in a Decomposer-Solver-Verifier 
pipeline and allow the next steps in the pipeline process to continue without change. Since 
the fault is purposely and known, every subsequent step is a result of an intentional fault. 
We have designed one of three operators to mimic a true reasoning failure. In the first 
operator, a number is substituted with a different but reasonable number (number swap), 
which can also be graded on a pre-established severity scale. The other two operators are an 
arithmetic operation reversal (operation flip) and an alteration of the logic or order of a 
reasoning step (semantic logic error). All fault injections are documented with the specific 
span that was altered.
Under 6 paired configurations (i.e., baseline, clean three-stage pipeline, injection at the 
decomposer and solver stages, verifier-removed versions, and two injected pipelines), our 
main experiments sample 200 GSM-Hard problems. Because all stage outputs are stored, we can 
replay the same experiment under different verifier prompts and compare verifier strategies 
with the same inputs. Currently, our code allows comparisons between direct verifiers, 
chain-of-thought verifiers, and rubric-based verifiers. We also conduct cross-model 
replication experiments on the Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct model to determine if the qualitative 
patterns of our main experiments are replicated in other model families. Comparisons are 
conducted at the level of individual problems and we report interval estimates and exact 
paired tests in addition to point estimates.
Our thesis is that in a Decomposer-Solver-Verifier pipeline, the probability of an injected 
fault surviving to the final answer is a function of the fault’s location and type, as well 
as the fault’s severity, the presence of a verifier, and the prompt given to the verifier. 
The design of our study tests each of these claims, particularly the effects of the stage (decomposer vs. solver), 
the verifier (verifier present vs. ablated), and the verifier-strategy (direct vs. chain-of-thought vs. rubric), 
on the injected fault, and the problem remains constant.
This study connects four areas of research. First, agentic frameworks like AutoGen~\citep{wu2023autogen}, 
MetaGPT~\citep{hong2023metagpt}, and CAMEL~\citep{li2023camel} have created reusable patterns for 
developing LLM agents. The Decomposer, Solver, and Verifier pipeline we examine is the 
simplest version that defines planning, execution, and review as separate roles.   
Second, previous work on debate, ensembling, and self-consistency measures reports accuracy gains~\citep{du2023debate,li2024moreagents,wang2022selfconsistency}, 
yet it typically does not separate systems that genuinely prevent errors from those that just 
substitute one error for another. Controlled fault injection makes it possible to observe this 
difference.   Third, research on self-correction and verification demonstrates both the potential 
and the limits of reflective reasoning. Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion} and Self-Refine~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine} 
show that models can improve outputs based on feedback. However, Huang et al.~\citep{huang2023selfcorrect} reveal that, 
without external signals, such revisions often struggle with reasoning tasks. Additionally, 
LLM judgments have known biases~\citep{zheng2023judge}. Therefore, our verifier ablation and 
replay design evaluate not just whether review happens but also how much it impacts the 
outcome of a known fault under the same conditions.   Finally, MAST~\citep{cemri2025mast} 
offers a terminology for multi-agent failure, while recent mitigation systems like DebUnc~\citep{yoffe2024debunc} and graph-based 
governance methods~\citep{xie2026spark} are based on the idea that upstream errors lead to 
downstream issues. Our study directly tests this idea and measures when it is valid.

\textbf{Contributions:}

(i)~A fault-injection approach with control over error propagation measurement within the 
agent pipeline of LLMs, which uses repeatable fault injection operators and pre-registered 
severity ranges.

(ii)~Quantitative measures of propagation, catching, and correction rates using paired 
comparisons, showing the effect of the injection stage, fault type, and fault severity.

(iii)~An ablation experiment with the verifier to measure the contribution of the verification
stage by comparing runs with and without verifiers.

(iv)~Strategy-level analysis of the verification stage through comparison of different 
verifier prompt strategies (direct, chain of thought, and rubric) using the same stored trace
for different fault types, severities, and injection stages.

(v)~Open source software that contains the prompts, configurations, analyses, and traces to 
reproduce our experiments.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Pipeline Architecture and Test Conditions}
Our multi-agent system uses three specialized LLM agents working sequentially:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decomposer:} Breaks down a word problem into a short 2--5 step plan without solving it.
    \item \textbf{Solver:} Executes the plan step-by-step to compute the final numeric answer.
    \item \textbf{Verifier:} Reviews the solution against the original problem and either accepts it or rejects it with a corrected answer.
\end{enumerate}

To isolate how errors propagate and how verifiers protect the pipeline, we test six experimental conditions on a fixed set of math problems (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). Condition~A is a single-agent baseline. Condition~B is a clean multi-agent run. In Conditions~C1 and C2, we inject a controlled fault into the plan or solution before passing it to the next agent. Conditions~D1 and D2 repeat C1 and C2 with the verifier removed to measure baseline error propagation.

\subsection{Controlled Fault Injection}
\label{sec:injection}
To simulate realistic reasoning mistakes, we corrupt exactly one intermediate artifact per run using three fault types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Number Swap:} Replaces a number in the plan or solution with a wrong value, categorized into three severity tiers: \emph{mild} ($<15\%$ change), \emph{moderate} ($30\%$--$60\%$ change), or \emph{severe} ($>200\%$ change or sign flip).
    \item \textbf{Operation Flip:} Swaps an arithmetic operation with its inverse (e.g., changing \emph{add} to \emph{subtract} or \emph{multiply} to \emph{divide}).
    \item \textbf{Semantic Logic Error:} Rewrites the logical sequence of steps while keeping the language fluent.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verifier Prompt Strategies and Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:strategies}
To test how prompt design affects verifier performance, we evaluate every run under three prompt strategies on identical traces:
(1)~\textbf{Direct:} Emits an immediate accept/reject verdict and corrected line;
(2)~\textbf{Chain-of-Thought (CoT):} Generates step-by-step verification reasoning before declaring a verdict; and
(3)~\textbf{Rubric:} Systematically evaluates the solution against four explicit criteria (reading, variables, operations, calculations).

Every injected fault results in one of four outcomes: \emph{caught and corrected}, \emph{caught but wrong correction}, \emph{silently propagated}, or \emph{absorbed} (the solver naturally corrected the plan mistake).

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.} Experiments are conducted on $n=200$ GSM-Hard math problems using Claude Sonnet 4.6 (primary) and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct (replication) at temperature 0. Condition comparisons use McNemar's exact test with Holm--Bonferroni correction ($p_{\mathrm{holm}}$). Prompts are listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prompts}.

\subsection*{RQ1: How do planning vs. execution errors propagate, and does removing the verifier increase failure?}

To begin with, injections in the planner agent (Condition C1) propagate to the final output in only 2.9\% of the cases on Claude and 5.6\% of them on Qwen. Solvers naturally absorb most plan errors by re-deriving calculations directly from the problem statement (Figure~\ref{fig:fate}). Solver-stage injections (Condition C2) behave differently across the two models: on Qwen they propagate at 15.9\%, roughly three times the plan-stage rate (OR = 0.32, $p = 0.0164$), whereas on Claude the two stages are indistinguishable at 2.9\% each. With only eight faults surviving across both Claude conditions, the Claude stage effect is better described as not estimable than as absent (OR = 0.94, 95\% CI [0.23, 3.86]); the evidence for a stage asymmetry therefore rests on the Qwen replication (full multivariable model in Table~\ref{tab:logit_master} and Figure~\ref{fig:forest}).

Removing the verifier agent (Condition D2) causes solver-stage fault propagation to jump from 2.9\% to 27.3\% on Claude ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} < 0.0001$) and from 15.9\% to 25.4\% on Qwen ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.5406$). In contrast, removing the verifier for plan-stage injections (Condition D1) increases propagation only modestly, to 8.6\% on Claude ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.1846$) and 8.7\% on Qwen ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 1.0000$), indicating that a \emph{single-pass} verifier adds little at the planning stage. This qualifier matters: under CoT and Rubric prompting the same Claude contrast is significant ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.0388$ and $0.0059$, Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_master}), so plan-stage verification is not worthless in general---it is Direct prompting specifically that fails to earn its place there. In other words, verifier agents provide a strong defense against late-stage execution errors---close to a ninefold reduction on Claude---while planning errors are largely self-corrected by intermediate agents before reaching the verification steps. The Qwen ablation moves in the same direction but does not reach significance.

\subsection*{RQ2: What are the effects of various verifier prompting strategies to catch errors with different levels of severity?}

Verifier prompt strategy matters considerably more on the weaker model than on the stronger one. On Claude, all three strategies already achieve high correction precision at the solver stage (95.7\% Direct, 98.0\% CoT, 100.0\% Rubric), and solver-stage propagation under Direct prompting is low to begin with (2.9\%), so Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Rubric buy only a further reduction to 0.7\%--1.4\%. On Qwen the gap is substantial: Direct prompting repairs only 78.5\% of the errors it catches and leaves 15.9\% of injected faults in the final answer, whereas CoT and Rubric raise correction precision to 95.3\%--98.0\% and cut propagation to 4.6\%--4.7\% (Table~\ref{tab:strategy}). This gain comes at the cost of a modest computational overhead: verifier-stage token usage rises by 52\%--96\%, but because verification is only one stage of the pipeline, total token cost per problem increases by just 23\%--37\% (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_reports}, Table~\ref{tab:master_cost} and Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} for full token breakdowns and the per-strategy cost/propagation trade-off).

\section{Analysis}

Qualitative trace inspection reveals two primary mechanisms governing verifier behavior and failure modes across the pipeline.

\subsection*{Post-Hoc Correction Drift in Direct Verification}
Direct verification shows a repair deficit that is pronounced on Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct and marginal on Claude Sonnet 4.6. Under Direct prompting the Qwen verifier identifies flawed solver outputs at a high rate (62.7\% catch at the solver stage) but repairs only 78.5\% of what it catches, against 98.0\% under Chain-of-Thought and 95.3\% under Rubric prompting. On Claude the same comparison spans a much narrower band, 95.7\%--100.0\%, so the bottleneck is a property of the weaker verifier rather than of single-pass prompting as such. The plausible mechanism is that Direct prompts require a verdict and a corrected answer in one pass, with no scratchpad in which to re-derive the value; Chain-of-Thought and Rubric prompting force an explicit derivation first. One caveat applies specifically to the Qwen traces: its Direct verifier frequently appends a ``Corrected line'' note \emph{after} its stated final answer, and some apparent repair failures are answer-extraction artifacts rather than arithmetic ones (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Under a stricter reading of those traces Direct correction precision rises to 84.8\%, still below both alternatives.

\subsection*{Label-Reasoning Dissociation}
A more subtle failure mode is \emph{label-reasoning dissociation}, where the verifier's written critique explicitly identifies the injected fault yet the final verdict token reads \texttt{VERDICT: ACCEPT}. For example, trace audits reveal instances where the verifier notes in prose that a step used an incorrect value, but nevertheless approves the overall solution. This dissociation breaks the implicit assumption that reasoning traces causally drive final verdicts. Structured Rubric prompting mitigates this dissociation by forcing the model to explicitly state pass/fail labels for individual evaluation criteria prior to emitting its overall verdict.

\section{Conclusion}
Errors do not travel equally across an agent pipeline. When a planner makes a mistake, the solver usually fixes it on its own by reading the original question. In fact, intermediate solvers absorb roughly 90\% of plan-stage errors across both Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct. Because of this self-correction, adding a verifier after the planner provides little value: under Direct verification it reduces final error propagation by 5.7 points on Claude (8.6\% D1 to 2.9\% C1) and 3.1 points on Qwen (8.7\% D1 to 5.6\% C1), neither of which is statistically significant. Under CoT and Rubric prompting the Claude plan-stage reduction does reach significance (Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar_master}), so the ``skip the planning verifier'' rule is specific to single-pass verification.

Solver errors are a completely different story. Mistakes made during execution pass straight to the final answer unless a verifier catches them. Here the verifier is doing most of the work that the pipeline does at all: propagation falls from 27.3\% without it to 2.9\% with it on Claude, a near-ninefold reduction ($p_{\mathrm{holm}} < 0.0001$). The Qwen replication moves the same way but is both smaller and not significant (25.4\% to 15.9\%, $p_{\mathrm{holm}} = 0.5406$), which is our clearest reminder that verifier value is model-dependent.

Prompt design matters, but less uniformly than we expected. On the weaker model the effect is large: Direct verifiers spot errors and then repair only 78.5\% of them, while Chain-of-Thought and Rubric prompting raise repair accuracy to 95.3\%--98.0\% and cut propagation from 15.9\% to 4.6\%--4.7\%. On the stronger model all three strategies already repair 95.7\%--100.0\% of what they catch at the solver stage (88.9\%--90.9\% at the planning stage), and the propagation difference between them (2.9\% versus 0.7\%--1.4\%) is small in absolute terms. Structured verification prompts are therefore most worth their extra tokens when the verifier is the weak link.

One major vulnerability remains. Simple operation flips---such as swapping \emph{add} for \emph{subtract}---act as a blind spot across all models and prompt styles, yielding catch rates below 15.0\%.

Our findings suggest three practical rules for building multi-agent systems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Verify execution, not planning:} Place verifiers at the solver stage where errors actually survive.
    \item \textbf{Match prompt structure to verifier strength:} Require step-by-step reasoning (Chain-of-Thought or Rubric) when the verifier is the weaker component, where it lifts repair precision from 78.5\% to 95.3\%--98.0\%. On a strong verifier, single-pass prompting already repairs 95.7\%+ and the extra tokens buy little.
    \item \textbf{Add symbolic safeguards:} Pair LLM verifiers with programmatic math checks to catch operation flips that language models miss.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Broader Impact.} Agent pipelines increasingly handle real-world tasks. Pinpointing where errors survive helps developers place guardrails effectively; we do not foresee direct misuse arising from these findings.

\section{Improvements}

\subsection*{Limitations of the present design}\label{sec:limitations}
Three limitations bound how far these results should be read.

\paragraph{Answer extraction is imperfect, and it is not neutral across models.}
Agents state their final answer in free text rather than a fixed field, so scoring requires
extracting a number from prose. We apply one deterministic rule to every trace: take the
number after the last \texttt{=} if the answer contains one, otherwise the first number in
the text. This rule was fixed before analysis and is applied blind, but it is not equally
suited to both models. Claude's verifier typically writes a bare expression
(``\texttt{36 + 54 = 90 seeds}''), which the rule reads correctly. Qwen's verifier often
states its answer first and then appends a note (``\texttt{1419161 (Corrected line:
5000 + 200 = 5200)}''), where the rule takes the number from the note. Auditing every
record on which the rule disagrees with the run-time score, we find it resolves 18 of 19
Claude cases correctly but only 4 of 10 Qwen cases. Re-scoring the six affected Qwen
records by hand moves Qwen's Direct solver-stage propagation from 15.9\% to 11.9\% and its
correction precision from 78.5\% to 84.8\% --- the direction of every conclusion above is
unchanged, but Qwen's Direct-strategy deficit is somewhat smaller than the headline numbers
suggest. We report the uniform rule throughout and flag this as measurement noise
concentrated in one model's Direct condition.

\paragraph{Claude retains too few surviving faults for stage-level inference.}
After verification, only eight injected faults survive across both Claude injection
conditions. That is enough to estimate an overall propagation rate but not enough to
estimate how stage or error type modulates survival: every Claude odds ratio in
Table~\ref{tab:logit_master} has a confidence interval spanning an order of magnitude in
both directions. Claims about \emph{which} faults survive therefore rest on the Qwen
replication, and should be treated as single-model findings until replicated on a third
system.

\paragraph{Synthetic faults, one task family.}
Injections are scripted perturbations of numbers and operators on grade-school math
problems. They are deliberately unambiguous so that survival is measurable, but they are
not a sample of the errors real pipelines make --- notably they exclude hallucinated
premises, retrieval failures, and tool-use errors, and they do not cover open-ended
generation where no gold answer exists.

\subsection*{Planned extensions}
Our disturbances are intentionally synthetic, which helps us pinpoint their source.
Naturally occurring errors are more varied. They require our operators to address mathematical 
mistakes and logical flaws instead of, for example, hallucinations during open-ended generations. 
We produce results based on two groups of models and one main task distribution that provide 
clear, verifiable outputs. We also understand that open-ended task dynamics may differ. The 
verifier prompts have been loosely designed, if at all, to optimize, and they represent only 
a rough configuration. The study was completed in four weeks. 
\newline The study could benefit in the following rough order of impact, based on the outlined 
extensions: expanding the protocol to other task distributions beyond GSM-Hard, broadening 
semantic fault injection to more systematically include contradictions and omissions as well 
as hallucinated constructs, refining the solver prompt to directly eliminate the absorption 
of planning stage faults through a silent re-derivation, thus improving the length estimation 
for the planning stage prompt, assessing how effectively an economical fine-tuning of a small 
verifier, using QLoRA~\citep{dettmers2023qlora}, impacts the provided tracing as a counterpart 
to the LLM judge, exploring the cost-benefit of token use with more complex verifiers where 
a chain of thought or rubric is only applied when the solver shows significant uncertainty, and 
investigating the DebUnc~\citep{yoffe2024debunc} model, which applies inter-agent Gating within 
our disturbance framework, creating a valuable connection between the mitigation structure and 
measurement discussed in this paper.

\section{Evaluation}
We assess the design against the standard threats to validity. \newline
\textbf{Internal validity}: All contrasts are paired on the same fixed,
seeded problem sample; every injection is validated and its full record
released, so any reader can audit the treatment. \newline \textbf{Construct validity}:
Propagation is measured only on problems the clean pipeline solves
correctly, so a wrong final answer under injection is attributable to the
fault rather than to baseline inability, and absorption is reported as its
own fate rather than folded into success. \newline \textbf{Statistical validity}: Exact
paired tests, Wilson and bootstrap intervals, and Holm correction over the
pre-registered contrast family~\citep{dror2018hitchhiker}. \newline \textbf{External
validity}: Results cover one primary reasoning dataset with objectively
checkable answers and two model families; replication on both Claude Sonnet
4.6 and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct tests whether the observed propagation
patterns depend on a single model. \newline All conclusions in this paper are drawn
from these deterministic primary runs (temperature 0). Where the two model families disagree
we say so explicitly rather than reporting the pooled result: the stage asymmetry is
established on Qwen and not estimable on Claude, the causal verifier effect at the solver
stage is significant on Claude but not on Qwen, and the Direct-strategy repair deficit is a
Qwen phenomenon. Only the plan-stage absorption result and the operation-flip blind spot hold
consistently across both.

\section{Role Prompts}\label{sec:appendix_prompts}

\paragraph{Decomposer.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a planning agent. Break the problem into a short numbered plan of 2-5 concrete steps with any intermediate quantities needed. Do NOT solve it. Output only the plan.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Solver.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a solving agent. Execute the given plan step by step on the problem. Show brief working, then end with the line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier (Direct).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a verification agent. Check the proposed solution against the original problem. If correct, reply 'VERDICT: ACCEPT'. If you find an error, reply 'VERDICT: REJECT', explain the error in one sentence, and give a corrected line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier (Chain-of-Thought).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a verification agent. Check the proposed solution against the original problem.\\
First, write down your step-by-step verification reasoning (thinking out loud). In your reasoning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carefully read the original problem statement to identify all numerical inputs, constraints, and implicit/explicit assumptions.
    \item Confirm if the proposed solution correctly read the problem and applied all necessary assumptions.
    \item Verify the mathematical logic and calculations step-by-step.
\end{itemize}
Then, end your response with:\\
VERDICT: ACCEPT (if the solution is correct)\\
OR\\
VERDICT: REJECT\\
If you reject, explain the error in one sentence, and give a corrected line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier (Rubric).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{You are a verification agent. Evaluate the proposed solution by systematically verifying these four items:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Reading \& Assumptions: Did the solution correctly read the problem text, capture all conditions, and apply correct assumptions?
    \item Variables: Are the numbers used from the question identical to the original problem?
    \item Operations: Are the mathematical operations chosen (add/sub/mult/div) logically correct?
    \item Calculations: Are the calculations mathematically correct?
\end{enumerate}
Write down your evaluation for each of the 4 points above.\\
Then, end your response with:\\
VERDICT: ACCEPT (if all checks pass)\\
OR\\
VERDICT: REJECT (if any check fails)\\
If you reject, explain the error in one sentence, and provide a corrected line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Single agent.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{Solve the problem step by step. End with the line: \texttt{FINAL ANSWER: <answer>}.}
\end{quote}

\section{Injection Examples}\label{sec:appendix_injections}

\paragraph{Number swap.}
``Step 1: add 45 and 12.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Step 1: add 76 and 12.''
(step labels unchanged).

``Take the 8 apples.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Take the 3 apples.''

``Compute 250 $\times$ 0.2.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Compute 150 $\times$ 0.2.''

\paragraph{Operation flip.}
``Multiply the total by 3.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Divide the total by 3.''

``Add the shipping cost at the end.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Subtract the shipping
cost at the end.''

``Divide the distance by the time.'' $\rightarrow$ ``Multiply the distance by
the time.''

\paragraph{Semantic logic error.}
``Use the discounted price first, then add the service fee.''
$\rightarrow$ ``Add the service fee first, then apply the discount to the total.''

``Since the boys and girls were split equally, divide the total by 2 before
applying the shell-count rule.''
$\rightarrow$ ``Apply the shell-count rule directly to the total before
splitting into groups.''

``Find the remaining amount after step 2, then compare it with the target.''
$\rightarrow$ ``Compare the amount with the target before subtracting the
intermediate cost.''

\section{Representative Run Trace}\label{sec:appendix_trace}
Below is a representative trace from our experimental runs (problem ID: \texttt{gsmhard-train-1209}), showing how a plan-stage number swap is propagated by the solver and subsequently corrected by the verifier:

\paragraph{Problem.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{The local firefighters are doing a ``fill the boot'' fundraiser. Their goal is to raise \$3522385. After the first 3 hours, they have raised \$2100. For how many hours do they have to fundraise in total to reach their goal, assuming an equal amount raised in every hour?}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Decomposer plan.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{1. Calculate the amount raised per hour by dividing \$2100 by 3 hours.\\
2. Divide the total goal (\$3,522,385) by the hourly rate to find the total number of hours needed.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Injected plan-stage fault (C1 condition - Number swap).}
\begin{quote}
\textit{1. Calculate the amount raised per hour by dividing \textbf{\$1890} by 3 hours.\\
2. Divide the total goal (\$3,522,385) by the hourly rate to find the total number of hours needed.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Solver execution.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{Step 1: Calculate the amount raised per hour: \$1890 $\div$ 3 = \$630 per hour.\\
Step 2: Divide the total goal by the hourly rate: \$3,522,385 $\div$ \$630 = 5,591.88... Rounding up to complete the goal: 5,592 hours.\\
FINAL ANSWER: 5592}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Verifier verdict \& correction.}
\begin{quote}
\textit{The problem states they raised \$2100 in the first 3 hours, giving a rate of \$2100 $\div$ 3 = \$700 per hour. The proposed solution incorrectly uses \$1890 and \$630 per hour instead. The correct calculation is \$3,522,385 $\div$ \$700 = 5,031.978..., which rounds up to 5,032 hours.\\
VERDICT: REJECT\\
FINAL ANSWER: 5032}
\end{quote}

\section{Answers to Omitted Research Sub-Questions}\label{sec:appendix_answers}

This appendix provides concise, point-form answers to the specific secondary research sub-questions that were streamlined out of the main text.

\subsection{Fault Severity \& Sensitivity Dynamics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Injection Stage Dominance:} The injection stage (planning vs. execution) is the primary determinant of fault propagation. Fault severity (mild, moderate, severe) plays a secondary, non-monotonic role.
    \item \textbf{Direct Verifier Sensitivity:} Under Direct verification, verifiers show no monotonic sensitivity to numerical slip magnitude. Solver-stage number-swap propagation on Claude is 0.0\% for mild, 12.5\% for moderate, and 0.0\% for severe; on Qwen it is 18.2\% mild, 33.3\% moderate, and 33.3\% severe. Each stratum contains only 11--18 injections, so these differences are not separable from sampling noise and we draw no ordering conclusion from them.
    \item \textbf{Structured Verifier Neutralization:} Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Rubric verification suppress number-swap propagation to \textbf{0.0\%} in 10 of 12 Claude severity strata and 9 of 12 Qwen strata. The exceptions are Rubric on moderate solver-stage swaps for Claude (6.7\%), and, for Qwen, CoT on moderate plan-stage swaps (5.6\%), CoT on severe solver-stage swaps (9.1\%), and Rubric on severe plan-stage swaps (12.5\%). Correction precision on caught number swaps is \textbf{100.0\%} across every Claude stratum (Table~\ref{tab:master_strategy}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational Overhead of Structured Verification}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Agent Baseline Overhead:} Moving from a single-agent baseline (Condition A) to a clean multi-agent pipeline (Condition B) increases total token cost per solved problem by \textbf{$3.1\times$} on Claude (526 to 1,616 tokens) and \textbf{$2.2\times$} on Qwen (737 to 1,623 tokens).
    \item \textbf{Verifier Stage Overhead:} Structured prompting (CoT and Rubric) increases token consumption at the verifier stage by \textbf{52\%--96\%} relative to Direct verification.
    \item \textbf{Pipeline Token Overhead:} Because verification represents only one stage of the pipeline, CoT and Rubric prompting add only a modest \textbf{24\%--37\%} total token cost per problem (Table~\ref{tab:master_cost}).
\end{itemize}
